## Summary
Participants saw security warnings inside an fMRI scanner, with eye tracking, over a five-day work week. Brain response and eye fixations dropped sharply over the week, with only partial recovery overnight. "Polymorphic" warnings, whose look changes each time, resisted habituation much better.

## Key points
- Habituation measured directly in the brain (repetition suppression) and with eye tracking at the same time.
- Attention fell across the week; it recovered only partly between days.
- Changing the warning's appearance slowed habituation for the whole five days.
- Eye tracking is a valid proxy for the neural measure, so cheaper studies can measure it.

## Excerpts
> "A major inhibitor of the effectiveness of security warnings is habituation: decreased response to a repeated warning." (Abstract)

> "Our results show a dramatic drop in attention throughout the workweek despite partial recovery between workdays." (Abstract)

> "Through this process—also known as warning blindness or fatigue—users' attention to warnings can attenuate to the point where they hardly see the warning any longer." (Introduction; citation markers removed)

## Relevance to the talk
Habituation is not laziness: it is the brain doing what it does to repeated stimuli. An agent that asks the same-looking question hundreds of times a day is training its user not to see it.
