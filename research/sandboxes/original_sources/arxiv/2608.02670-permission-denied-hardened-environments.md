## Summary
Coding-agent benchmarks normally run in permissive sandboxes, but real enterprises impose scoped credentials, restricted egress, read-only filesystems and non-root execution. Evaluating 12 coding agents on Terminal-Bench 2.1 under nested policy levels shows hardening always costs something, by up to 18.3 points of success and 167.3% in cost, and the cost differs widely by model. Agents rarely stop cleanly when blocked; they grind into timeouts or wrong answers.

## Key points
- 12 agents, Terminal-Bench 2.1, nested real-world-derived security policy levels.
- Strictest policy: success loss up to 18.3 points; cost inflation up to 167.3%.
- Success and efficiency disagree: the model that best keeps success loses the most efficiency, so model choice depends on policy.
- Failure mode under restriction: timeouts/wrong solutions rather than early stop.
- Releases Boundary-Bench, a hardening plugin for Terminal-Bench-compatible benchmarks.

## Excerpts
> "Existing benchmarks, however, evaluate agents almost exclusively in permissive sandboxes, so it is unknown how performance changes when policy is enforced." (Abstract)

## Relevance to the talk
Puts a number on the usability tax of tight sandboxes for coding agents, the trade-off that drives products toward permissive defaults.
